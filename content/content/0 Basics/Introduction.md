My introduction to fresh pasta was during the first time I was living by myself.  I had moved away from home and was in my own condo in south Florida.  A close friend and her husband went on a trip to Italy and did a pasta class while there.  The photos and instructions she sent back from the class inspired me to try to make some myself.  I was armed with all purpose flour, eggs, and a rolling pin.  Following the instructions, which were no more detailed than an infographic, I made pasta that was entirely too thick and were more like the dumplings from chicken and dumpling than they were spaghetti noodles.  They still tasted good but I knew that I had not done well.  Like many other cooking experiments that one does when they live alone, I appreciated that I had tried and failed, ate the monstrosity anyways because I am too cheap to just throw it away, and did not make a second attempt.  It was not until years later, when living with my then-boyfriend-now-husband, that I tried again.

We decided to take a cooking class together.  It was one of those fun things that couples do, and we were a couple and fun, so one evening we found ourselves at the back of "Sur La Table" to learn how to make pasta.  My husband and I are both very good cooks (I had improved since my first failed pasta experiment) and bonded over our love of food.  We both have learned a lot about cooking from each other and tried new recipes together.  It was hard to pick a cooking class, as many classes covered techniques or dishes we already knew.  A class on cooking steak?  We already had that covered.  A class on sushi?  Noah doesn't like fish.  A class on baking pies?  I am the resident pastry chef that is making pies each holiday.  Pasta was going to be new ground for both of us.

The pasta we made in that class turned out great, and I still have those recipes, however, since continuing my pasta education I have learned that our teacher missed some details.  We got a nice brief overview using premeasured ingredients and a guiding hand.  A nice introduction for sure.  But when home and trying to make pasta again, I realized that there was more to it.  Not as an elaborate and complicated creation, but as a practice in being aware of the texture and moisture level of the dough.  It was not until I did some research on why I was having trouble rolling the dough at home I realized that the teacher never had us knead the dough long enough.  And this was the start of the pasta madness.  When exploring youtube videos on pasta making, I learned that there are different types of doughs, different shapes. That pasta could be a very complex and long dish, such as a short rib ravioli, or very simple, like aglio e olio (this is pasta that is made with only olive oil \[olio] and garlic \[algio]). Then tangentially to pasta was a whole tree of different sauces to be learned. I love learning and pasta evolved into a new hobby for me where I could explore these different shapes and sauces.

The purpose of this guide is to clearly outline what I have learned so that you can more easily navigate the overwhelming options of pasta doughs, shapes, and sauces.  This guide grew from my own collection of notes on this topic that I compiled using the software Obsidian (www.obsidian.md).  These notes were necessary to keep track of my favorite recipes and the edits that I made to them.  After working from many pasta books and youtube videos, I needed a place to house the condensed knowledge that I squeezed from them so that I wasn't having to flip through several different books again.

These notes are organized by the different phases of a pasta dish:
Dough: Which includes notes on different types of flour and includes several different dough recipes for difference purposes.  While egg doughs are the most commonly seen type of fresh pasta, dough made with only semolina flour and water is better for certain shapes and dishes.  Why is there a whole section for dough?  Well, we get a little weird in there. Want some gluten free pasta?  Or green pasta?  Or green pasta with yellow stripes?  Or purple gnocchi?

Shapes: Pasta can come in a wide variety of shapes.  Sheets for lasagna, thin spaghetti noodles, bowties, gnocchi, bucatini, suprasine.  I could keep going.  This section outlines the shapes and includes photos and videos for guidance and a short list of recommended doughs to use when making them.
